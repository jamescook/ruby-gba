# DSL reference: Sound & music

The whole story of these verbs. The one-line index is `.claude/rules/dsl-reference.md`.

- `enable_sound`, `define_sound :name, frequency:, duty:, decay:, volume:`, `beep :tone` — SFX (channel 2).
- `noise :explosion` (also `:hit`/`:kick`/`:snare`/`:hat`/`:zap`, or `pitch:`/`decay:`/`volume:`/`metallic:`) — a percussion/explosion hit on the noise voice (channel 4).
- `wave :triangle, :C4` (shapes `:sine`/`:triangle`/`:sawtooth`/`:square`; pitch a note or Hz; `volume:` `:full`/`:three_quarter`/`:half`/`:quarter`/`:mute`), `stop_wave` — a sustained richer-timbre tone on the wave voice (channel 3). **Or give the waveform itself**, as 32 steps of 0 to 15, wherever one of those names goes — `wave PULSE_50, :C4`, `plays: PULSE_50`. The names are a convenience rather than the truth: this voice is not a set of timbres, it is 32 steps of four bits that the game writes, so music that came from somewhere else — decoded out of another cartridge, drawn in a tracker — arrives holding those 32 numbers and no name would be the waveform it actually has. A 50% pulse (`[15] * 16 + [0] * 16`) is the common one and is none of the four; played as a triangle it is a different instrument, which a player hears without being able to name. Name it in your own program and the call site still reads like game code. A list that is not 32 long, or a step outside 0 to 15, is a friendly error at the line that wrote it.
- `song(:name) { … }`, `play_song :name`, `stop_music` — music. Inside `song`: `tempo`, `note :C4, :quarter`, `rest :quarter`, `duty`, `volume`. **A note on a square or the noise voice can fade out or swell in by itself** — `decay :slow` in a part fades every note it plays, a plucked string or a bell, and `swell :medium` brings every note in, a bowed one or a pad that breathes (start it at `volume 0` and it rises out of nothing). The speeds are the ones `beep` and `noise` take, `:fast`, `:medium`, `:slow` or `:none`, or the step itself, 0 to 7 — the 64ths of a second the voice holds each step of loudness for, which is what music converted from another game's arrives holding. One note can say its own, with its own volume: `note :C4, :quarter, decay: 2, volume: 9`. The voice counts the fade itself, so it costs nothing a frame, and it is the same circuit on the Advance and the Color. A square part that says nothing holds each note until the next, as it always did; a drum part that says nothing rings out `:fast`. Saying it of a part on the wave voice or one that plays a recording is a friendly error (the wave voice has four loudnesses and no counting; a recording's shape is its `envelope:`), and so is a note that says both. **The music volume reaches such a note only as it starts**: `music_volume` and the music fades strike a held note again at the new volume, which would start a fading note's fade over, so a note that fades by itself is left to it, the way a drum hit is, and the part's next note takes the new volume. **A square note that swells stops at the music volume**: the voice counts it up as far as full, so in a game that says `music_volume`, the music player counts along with it, and when the note reaches the loudest the music volume lets a note be (15 times the volume, over 100), strikes it again there, holding. As the music fades the note is struck lower each time the volume drops below it, and when the volume comes back up it is struck again where it was held, swelling on. The interpreter's log says each strike as `[:loudness, voice, volume]`, with the swell after it, `NoteFade.swelling(step)`, when it swells on. It costs a game that moves the music volume and has a swelling square note a few instructions a frame for each square voice; any other game, nothing. A swelling drum hit still climbs to full. The interpreter's log names a note's fade at the end of its `[:voice, …]` entry when the note or its part said one. Worked examples: `examples/chip_band.rb` (the same tune held and faded, for the Advance and the Color) and `test/all/vblank/audio/test_song_note_fades.rb`. **A note on the song's first square part can slide its pitch by itself** — `note :C5, :eighth, slide_up: :fast` is a zap, and `slide_down :slow` in a part swoops every note it plays down. The first square voice has a circuit the others lack: every so many 128ths of a second it moves the note's pitch by a part of itself. The speeds are `:fast`, `:medium` and `:slow` (a step every 1, 3 or 7 128ths, each moving the voice's pitch number by a 32nd of itself), or the voice's own two numbers, `slide_up: { every: 1..7, shift: 1..7 }` — the 128ths each step waits, and how far right the pitch number is shifted to make the step (1 is half of it, 7 a 128th), which is what music converted from another game arrives holding (a pitch sweep's time and shift; its time's fourth bit means nothing to the voice). `slide_down: :none` holds one note's pitch in a part that slides, and asks nothing of the voice, so any part can say it. Sliding up climbs faster and faster, and once the next step would run past the top of what the voice can play the voice switches itself off: the zap ends in silence. Sliding down slows as it falls and never runs out. It costs nothing a frame, and it is the same circuit on the Advance and the Color. A Score's `Part` and `Note` take `slide_up:` and `slide_down:` too. **Only the song's first square part can**: a slide said by its second square part is a build error that says to put the part before the other one, and one said by a part on the wave or noise voice, or one that plays a recording, is a friendly error, and so is a note that says both ways. The music volume leaves a sliding note alone, as it does a fading one. The interpreter's log names a note's slide after its fade in its `[:voice, …]` entry (a nil fade before it when the note said none). On the Color a game whose music slides a note takes a byte more for every note, and a game that slides none takes nothing. Worked examples: `examples/chip_band.rb` (SLIDING NOTES) and `test/all/vblank/audio/test_song_pitch_slides.rb`. **A note on a square or the wave part can wobble its pitch** — vibrato: `vibrato depth: :slight, speed: :medium, after: :eighth` in a part wobbles every note it plays, and `note :A5, :whole, vibrato: :none` holds one still (or `vibrato: { depth: 5 }`, its own). No voice wobbles by itself, so the music player does it, a frame at a time and the same on every backend: once the note has sounded for `after` frames (a number up to 255, or a note length at the song's tempo; nought unless said), the voice's pitch number goes up by half the `depth` (rounded up) and holds there for `speed` frames, then down by the other half below where the note began for `speed` frames, and back, until the part's next note. The pitch number is the one the voice is tuned by, so a step is the voice's own smallest change of pitch, larger on a high note. `depth` is `:slight`, `:medium` or `:wide` (2, 4 or 8) or 1 to 15 steps, and `speed` is `:fast`, `:medium` or `:slow` (2, 4 or 8) or 1 to 16 frames; what a Hash leaves out is `:medium`, `:medium` and nought — so music converted from another game's engine gives the three numbers it has, a vibrato's delay, extent and rate (the rate plus one: the engine counts it down to nought). The note is not started again as its pitch moves, so its loudness and fade carry on, and a new music volume reaches it as it does any held note. A Score's `Part` and `Note` take `vibrato:` too, its wait in frames. Saying it of a part on the noise voice or one that plays a recording is a friendly error, and so is a note that both slides and wobbles, since both move the same pitch. The interpreter's log names a note's vibrato after its slide in its `[:voice, …]` entry, and says each turn to the other side as `[:wobble, voice, steps]`. It costs a game whose notes wobble a few instructions a frame for each voice that can, and a word more a note on the Advance and two bytes more on the Color; a game with none, nothing. Worked examples: `examples/chip_band.rb` (WOBBLING NOTES) and `test/all/vblank/audio/test_song_vibrato.rb`. **A part can play from one speaker** — `pan :left` in a part plays every note it plays from the left speaker alone, `pan :right` from the right, and `pan :center` (what a part that says nothing does) from both; a note says its own with `note :C5, :quarter, pan: :right`, so a part moves from side to side as the song goes. The console sends each of its voices to the left, the right or both by a pair of switches, and the music player sets a voice's as each note starts: a rest sends it back to both, and so does the game's own `beep`, `noise` and `wave` on that voice. `enable_sound` sends every voice to both. The square parts, the wave part and the drums can; a part that plays a recording cannot, since every recording is mixed into the one stream both speakers play, and saying it there is a friendly error. A Score's `Part` and `Note` take `pan:` too. The interpreter's log names a note's speaker last in its `[:voice, …]` entry, `:left` or `:right`, and nothing for both. The desktop, web and Ruby builds play in stereo. It costs a game whose notes pan a few instructions a note and a word more a note on the Advance (the one a wobble takes, when it has one), and a byte more a note on the Color; a game with none, nothing. Worked examples: `examples/chip_band.rb` (LEFT AND RIGHT) and `test/all/vblank/audio/test_song_pan.rb`. **A square note can have a shape of its own, or shapes that change while it holds** — `note :C5, :quarter, duty: :eighth` plays one note thin in a part that is hollow, and `duty %i[eighth quarter half quarter]` in a part (or `duty:` with a list on a note) steps every note it plays through those shapes, one a frame, round and round until the part's next note: the shimmer other games' music engines make. A list is two shapes, which take turns, or four; any other length is a friendly error. The note's first frame plays its first shape. A square voice takes a new shape at once without starting its note again, so the note's loudness, fade and pitch carry on, and the music volume striking a held note again puts it back in the shape of its frame before the frame is heard. A rest, the game's own `beep` on the second voice, an effect's end and a song stopping each stop the stepping. Only a square part has a shape: `duty:` on a note of a part on the wave or noise voice, or one that plays a recording, is a friendly error. A Score's `Part` and `Note` take `duty:` too, a shape or a list. The interpreter's log names the shapes a note or its part said last in its `[:voice, …]` entry (an `Audio::Duty`; nothing for a part that names one shape, as before), and each change of a stepping shape as `[:duty, voice, shape]`. A note's own single shape costs nothing on the Advance (its row holds it already) and a byte a note on the Color; a game whose notes step their shapes pays a few instructions a frame for each square voice, and a word more a note on the Advance; a game with none, nothing. Worked examples: `examples/chip_band.rb` (SHIMMERING NOTES) and `test/all/vblank/audio/test_song_duties.rb`. **A part can play higher or lower than it is written** — `transpose 12` in a part plays every note an octave up and `transpose(-7)` a fifth down (whole semitones, up to 48 either way), and `pitch_offset 2` plays every note two of the voice's own pitch steps sharp (`-2` flat): the number a square voice or the wave voice is tuned by, where each step up is a wave a tick of its clock shorter, so a step is small on a low note and larger on a high one. That is how another game's music engine detunes one part against another, and two parts playing the same notes a step or two apart beat together like a chorus. A part says both, transposed first. Both are worked into each note's pitch as the game is built, so every backend plays the same pitches and a frame costs nothing more. Any part can transpose; a pitch offset on the drums or a part that plays a recording is a friendly error (neither has pitch steps), and so is an offset that moves a note past what its voice can play, which names the note. A Score's `Part` takes `transpose:` and `pitch_offset:` too. The interpreter's log names each note at the pitch it plays. Worked examples: `examples/chip_band.rb` (DETUNED NOTES) and `test/all/vblank/audio/test_song_pitch_shifts.rb`. **`loop_from_here`** marks where the song comes round to at its end, so what is written before it — a fanfare, an introduction — plays once and what follows repeats; mark it in one part, or at the same moment in every part (two different moments is a friendly error, and so is a mark with nothing after it). **A repeat with no note in it is a friendly error too** — a mark followed only by rests, where the song would play its introduction once and then fall silent for as long as the game plays it; a note held across the mark counts, since it sounds on into the repeat. Coming round, every part is put the way it was at that moment the first time: a part holding a note across the mark sounds that note again there (not on the first pass, where it is already sounding — and not at all if the song ends on that very note, which then simply carries on), and a part silent there is silenced there, whatever the end of the song left it doing. None of that costs anything a frame; a part holding a note across the mark costs a second copy of its notes after it, in the cartridge. Layer parts with `voice :melody do … end` / `voice :bass do … end` — the framework picks where each one plays. **A part can play a recorded instrument instead of the square wave**: `voice :melody, plays: :piano do … end`, where `:piano` is an `instrument` (its name or the handle). Each note plays the recording at that note's pitch, a rest silences it, and a note ends early if the recording runs out first — unless the recording says where a held note reads back to (`holds_from:`, see `instrument`). The part names the instrument, never a channel, and it sounds through the mixer on a voice of its own. **A part can shape its notes too** — `voice :melody, plays: :piano, envelope: { release: 0.25 } do … end`, and one note of it can say its own (`note :C4, :quarter, envelope: { release: 0.6 }`) for a note plucked where the rest are bowed. A part that says nothing takes whatever the instrument itself was declared with, which is where music decoded from elsewhere keeps it; see `instrument` for what the four words mean and why a note that stops dead clicks. A shape costs nothing per note in the cartridge, because a note already names the recording it plays and two notes on the same recording shaped differently are simply two entries in the table it names. **Or one of the console's own other two voices, and those cost NO mixer voice at all** — `voice :pad, plays: :wave do … end` and `voice :drums, plays: :noise do … end`. The **wave** voice loops a short waveform, so it is rounder than a square wave and reaches an octave lower: a pad, a bell, a bass. Name a timbre instead and you pick the waveform (`plays: :sine`, `:triangle`, `:sawtooth`; bare `:wave` is the triangle) — or give the 32 steps themselves (`plays: PULSE_50`), for music that arrived with a waveform none of the names is; see `wave` above. The **noise** voice makes a hiss rather than a pitched tone, which is the drums — a note's pitch is how HIGH the hiss sits, so a low note is a kick and a high one a hat, and the part says how fast a hit fades (`decay :fast`) and whether it rattles (`metallic true`). Why reach for these before another recording: the console makes both sounds itself, where every recorded part keeps one of the mixer's voices for as long as its note sounds — so a song with a pad and drums leaves fifteen of the sixteen free for the game's own sounds. There is one of each voice, so one part apiece. And `plays: :square` is a friendly error, because it reads two ways and the wrong reading is silent: a part plays the square wave when it names no instrument, so that is `plays:` left out. **How many parts a song can have**: 2 square-wave parts — the console has two square-wave voices for music — plus one on the wave voice and one on the noise voice, plus up to 16 that play an instrument, one per voice of the framework's mixer. That 16 is the framework's, not the console's: recordings are mixed in software, and 16 is how many the mixer sums today — a voice costs mixing only while it sounds, so the number is room rather than a price. Going past any of them is a friendly error naming the one you hit, and pointing at whichever voices still have room — checked on the finished program, so a `songs` list of Scores meets it the same as a `song` block. **A square-wave part cannot go below 64 Hz**, just under C2: a lower note is played AT 64 Hz, a different note, so it is a friendly warning naming the note and the lowest one that sounds right (music decoded off another cartridge, which played the note at 64 Hz too, can say so — see `low_notes_intended:` under `songs`). The note names stop at C2 and never reach it; a pitch in Hz or a Score's MIDI key can. A part on the wave voice goes an octave lower, and a part that plays an instrument has no such floor at all — those are the two ways to a bass that goes deeper. **A sound effect and a song part on the same voice interrupt each other**, which is a friendly warning naming the song: `beep` shares the voice a song's SECOND square part uses, and `wave` and `noise` share the one voice each of theirs uses — so a single part on either already collides, where one square part leaves the beep alone. And each part plays at most one note a frame, so **a note shorter than a frame** (a sixteenth at a tempo near 2000) would leave the note after it unplayable and the part silent until the song comes round — that is a friendly error saying which part and how far in. **The music and the game's own sounds share the mixer's voices**, each taking one only while it sounds: a recorded part's note takes a voice and its rest gives it back, so while a part rests — and on a title screen with no song at all — every voice is the game's. When they run out, **a stated rule decides who gives way**, the same on both backends. A song's note that has ended and is still fading away (see `envelope:` on `instrument`) gives way first, to anybody, the quietest of them first — it is on its way out already. Past that, a game `play` that finds no voice free is dropped, as it always was, but a song's note that finds none takes the voice of the game's sound that has been playing longest — one that plays once before one that loops, since a loop never ends by itself — so a song keeps playing right and an older sound is cut short. A sound effect's note does the same, and in a game whose effects play recordings there is one step more: with no game sound to take, a note takes the voice of the lowest-priority note of a song or an effect ranked below its own (see `sound_effects`), and with none of those it is not played — its part carries on in time and is heard again from its next note, and `rom.profile` counts it with the sounds that did not play. A part's next note takes over the voice its last note was using, rather than a second one — **unless that note has a shape**: then it fades away on its own voice while the next note takes another, because taking its voice over would stop the old wave dead mid-fade, which is the click the shape was there to remove. So a shaped part can hold a second voice for as long as a fade lasts, and a game that shapes nothing shares its voices exactly as before. A recorded part also leaves the square voice beeps use alone. Naming an instrument the game never declared is a friendly error. What it costs is measured: `rom.profile` names the mixing, which grows with each recorded note sounding, and the routine that answers the display, which plays the music. **`play_song` says which song is playing now**, the way `show_map` says which map is showing: the song plays from its start and loops, and saying the one already playing changes nothing — so it can be written once, or every frame, from a branch or a scene, and it is the same tune. Naming a different song starts that one from its beginning (a jukebox needs no `stop_music` between tunes), and `stop_music` silences the song playing; said again while nothing plays, it does nothing, so a "music off" setting can say it every frame without touching the voice sound effects play on. **A song named starts on the NEXT frame**: the player moves the music on as the screen finishes a picture, which is before the game's pass, so it hears the name a frame on. A cartridge whose own sound engine runs after its game's logic has the song a frame sooner than that, so a game rebuilt to match one — asking for each song on that cartridge's own frames — says **`play_song :theme, this_frame: true`** (and `music.play 2, this_frame: true`): the song is taken up and its first frame played right where it is named, with the screen's interrupt held off while it is, and the next frame plays its second. Every note is then a frame sooner, and the player's own record names the song on the frame the game named it. Naming the song already playing changes nothing either way, and a number naming no song in a list leaves the music as it is. It costs a call where it is said and a second copy of the player's frame of the tune, beside the player, which the game calls: measured at 740 bytes for a game with square and recorded parts, and it grows with the kinds of part a game's songs have. A game that never says it emits neither. Anything but `true` or `false` is a friendly error. **It keeps its tempo whatever the game is doing**: the framework moves the song on once for every frame the screen shows, off the screen's own clock as the mixer is, so a game running at thirty frames a second still hears its music at the speed it was written. That place is the screen finishing a picture, so a program that never waits for the screen has no frames and plays no song — a friendly warning. What it costs is a check per part on every frame a song plays, whatever its length, and a handful of instructions on a frame no song plays.
- `music_volume 50` — **how loud the music plays**, from 0 (silent) to 100 (as written), and it may be something the game works out (`music_volume slider`, for a settings screen). It reaches the song playing now — notes already sounding included, from the next frame — and every song after it until it is said again; anything past either end is held at that end. Sound effects are not the music and keep their own volume. **Every kind of part follows it**, each the way its voice allows: a held square-wave note is started again at the new volume (those voices take a volume only as a note starts, which is also how Nintendo's own sound engine changes one), the wave voice and a recorded part's mixer voice simply take the new volume while they play (the wave voice has five volumes, so it gets the nearest), and a drum hit on the noise voice keeps ringing at the volume it was struck with and the next hit takes the new one — striking it again would be a second hit. **What it costs** is nothing for a game that never says it; for one that does, a check each frame, and on a frame it moved, the held notes set again. **With no number it reads how loud the music is now**, 0 to 100, which is how a game waits for a fade to finish: `(music_volume == 0).then { track.set! next_track; fade_music_in }` switches songs while nobody can hear it.
- `fade_music_out` / `fade_music_in` (both take `frames:` or `duration:` in seconds, half a second by default) — **the music going down with the lights, and coming back up.** The pair to `fade_out`/`fade_in`, and said the same way: call each once, at the moment it begins, and the framework walks `music_volume` every frame from there. `fade_music_in` with no length comes up as fast as the music last went down. **A faded-out song goes on playing, silently** — the way a faded-out screen goes on being drawn — so what happens while nobody can hear it is the game's to say: stop it, switch to another song, or bring the same one back. That is also why a jukebox that names its song every frame can fade between songs without the old one starting over. `fade_music_in` brings the music up from wherever it is — after a fade out, from silence — and with the music already at full it has nothing to do. Calling either again while a fade runs turns it round from where it is, and a fade that is not running starts from the volume the music is at, including one the game set by hand. **A beep on the second square voice during a fade** is cut off each time the volume steps, since a held note on that voice is started again at every step — the same voice already interrupts beeps whenever a two-part song plays a note on it (see the song line), and a fade does it more often. A `fade_music_out` with no `fade_music_in` (or `music_volume` turning it back up) anywhere is a friendly warning, because every song after it plays at no volume. Worked examples: `examples/jukebox.rb` (moving the cursor fades one tune out and the next in) and `examples/pong.rb` (the music fades as the game ends, and a new game fades it back in).
- `sample :name, from: "clip.wav"` (or `pcm: [-128..127…], rate: N`, and `note:` — the pitch it was recorded at, default `:C4`) → a `Sample`: a recorded 8-bit PCM clip played through the sampled-audio hardware (Direct Sound), not a synthesized PSG tone. `from:` loads a `.wav` (any 8-/16-bit, mono/stereo PCM — converted for you, keeping its rate; file found beside your script); `pcm:` takes raw samples (an Array of -128..127 or a byte `String`) at `rate:` (default 8192). `s.play` plays it once; `s.play(loop: true)` replays it seamlessly as looping background music (any length — it streams from ROM a frame at a time); `s.play(volume: :half)` sets its level (`:full`/`:three_quarter`/`:half`/`:quarter`/`:mute`); `s.play(pitch: :E4)` plays it at a different note by reading through it faster/slower (one recorded note covers a whole keyboard — shifts from the sample's `note:`); `s.stop` cuts its voices off. **Several samples play at once** — a software mixer sums the sounding voices (background music under a handful of overlapping effects, or chords), up to 16 voices, shared with a song's recorded parts (see the song line for who gives way when they run out); a play that finds none free takes the voice of a song's or a sound effect's note that is fading away, and with none of those is dropped rather than stealing one. **A recording cannot be named `:sine`, `:triangle`, `:sawtooth`, `:wave`, `:noise` or `:square`** — a friendly error — because `plays:` reads those as the console's own voices, so a song part could never play a recording by that name, and it would sit in the cartridge unplayed with nothing to say so. **What a voice costs is the mixing, and only while it sounds**: each one sounding is about 11 instructions for every sample of sound, so at around 8000 samples a second a frame spends about a hundredth of itself on each — twice that at twice the rate — and a frame with nothing sounding costs next to nothing. `rom.profile` names the mix routine and its share. **The rate it all comes out at is the framework's to pick, not yours**, and this is the one place that shows: `rate:` says what a recording was MADE at, and the framework then plays everything at a single rate it works out from your recordings. It cannot be any rate at all — the sound hardware has to be clocked so that a whole number of samples, and a whole number of the lots its transfer engine moves, fits exactly in one frame, or every frame hands over mid-sample and the game plays with a rattle under it at the frame rate. So it picks the nearest rate that does fit, your recordings are resampled to it as they play (at the pitch and for the duration they were recorded at, so nothing changes but a little bandwidth or a little mixing), and `rom.profile` says which rate you got. Nothing in the program names it. The framework hides the FIFO/DMA/timer/mixing; sound needs a `game_loop` (the mixer refills once a frame) and reserves two hardware timers — one clocks the sound, one counts how much of it has played — so user `timer`s allocate from timer 2. **The count is what keeps the sound whole in a game too busy for its frame**: the hand-over to the sound hardware waits for anything the game is in the middle of (a whole-screen clear is a fifth of a frame), and a different wait every frame used to be a click every frame. The count says exactly how much sound went out meanwhile, and each frame's sound carries enough of the next one's start to cover a third of a frame of lateness. Past that, the sound clicks at that frame and picks up again.
- `music = songs :music, [score, …]` (or a Hash, `{ title: TITLE, forest: FOREST }`) → a `SongList`: **music handed over as data and played by number**, for a game whose music already exists as numbers — decoded out of another cartridge, read from a file — rather than written as `song` blocks. `music.play 2`, `music.play :forest`, or `music.play track` with a number the game works out; it says which song is playing now, exactly like `play_song` — `this_frame: true` included — and a worked-out number naming no song leaves the music as it is (the same as `show_map`). A written number or name the list does not have is a friendly error. `music.stop` is `stop_music`. Each entry is a `VBlank::Audio::Score`: `Score.new(parts: [Score::Part.new(plays: :flute, notes: [Score::Note.new(at: 0, key: 72, length: 22), …])], tempo: 150)`. **Time is in ticks, the way sequenced music is stored everywhere**: `ticks_per_beat:` (24 unless said) and `tempo:` in beats a minute — or a map, `[[0, 120], [960, 140]]`, for a tempo that changes — and the framework turns ticks into frames once, at build time, from exact fractions, so a long song never drifts. A note's `key:` is a MIDI note number (60 is middle C) or a name like `:C4`, or nothing for a rest. **A note on a noise part can give `hz:` instead**, how many times a second its hiss steps, from just above 0 up to 524,288: the highest key is 12,543 Hz, and most drums a cartridge's sound engine plays step faster than that (131,072 is common). It plays the rung of the noise voice's ladder nearest that rate, as a key does. `hz:` on any other part, beside a `key:`, or outside that range is a friendly error (`test/all/vblank/audio/test_scores.rb`). **A note on a square part or the wave part can give `pitch_number:` instead**, 0 to 2047: the number the voice itself is tuned by, the same steps `pitch_offset` counts. Another Game Boy game's music engine keeps a table of these, and many of its notes are a step from the pitch a key comes to, so music converted from it says the number and sounds the same; the Game Boy Color and the Advance play exactly that number, and any other console the pitch nearest it. A part's `pitch_offset` and `transpose` still move it. `pitch_number:` on a noise or recording part, beside a `key:`, or outside 0 to 2047 is a friendly error (`test/all/vblank/audio/test_song_pitch_shifts.rb`). `length:` ends it early (otherwise it lasts until the part's next note), and it can name its own `instrument:`, `volume:` (0–15) and `envelope:`, so a part can change instrument, loudness or shape note by note — and its own `decay:` or `swell:`, how a note on a square or the noise voice fades out or swells in (see `song`), which a `Part` takes too for all its notes. **On a part that plays a recording, a volume can hold a fraction** (`volume: 7.5`, or a Rational), and that note plays at a loudness between two whole volumes: the mixer has 512 steps where a console voice has 16, and `15 × L / 512` gives a note a loudness of exactly L 512ths of the recording's size, which its shape, if it has one, then scales. That is the scale a cartridge's own sound engine keeps a voice at — it mixes by the SUM of the voice's right and left volumes — so music decoded off one hands over that sum as L, keeps its loudest notes, about half the recording, at that level and its quiet ones heard, where rounding them to 16 steps would lose them. A volume that comes to less than half a 512th is a rest. A volume with a fraction on a square, wave or noise part is a friendly error, since those voices have 16 volumes; and a `song` block's `volume` stays a whole number, so music that wants the finer steps is handed over as a Score. A `Part` takes `envelope:` too, for the whole line of music, and either takes the same two forms `instrument` does — a Hash of times, or the four numbers the console's own engine keeps, which is what a decoder already has. `length:` on the Score is where it comes round again, and left out it is where the last note ends. **`loop_from:` is the tick it comes round TO** — `loop_from: 384` plays the first 384 ticks once, as an introduction, and everything after over and over; left out, the whole song repeats. One tick serves the whole Score, since every part comes round at the same moment. **Music decoded off a cartridge can say its low notes are meant** — `Score.new(..., low_notes_intended: true)`. A square-wave note below 64 Hz is played at 64 Hz, and the build warns about one because in music written by hand it is a mistake. A cartridge's own sound engine plays such a note at 64 Hz too, so music lifted off one is already faithful, and every fix the warning offers would make it less so. The word leaves out that one warning for that one Score, and builds the same cartridge to the byte; a hand-written part in the same game still warns. A tick at or past the end, or one with no note sounding after it, is a friendly error — the second because the song would fall silent after its first time through. See the song line above for what a part holding a note across that point does. **A song that ends says `plays_once: true`**, and then it does not come round at all. It lasts exactly its length; on the frame after its last, its parts go quiet the way `music.stop` silences them, and nothing plays until the game says otherwise. Music decoded off a cartridge knows which kind each song is — a track there ends with a mark that sends it back or with one that stops it — so a title jingle, or the tune a legend is told under, stops where the original's does. It is counted on the frames the screen shows, like the rest of the music, which a game counting frames beside the song cannot do on a screen that runs late. `play` still says which song is playing now, so a game that names the song on every pass hears it once: naming the song already named changes nothing, ended or not. To hear it again, stop the music and name it (`music.stop` and `music.play :legend`, in one pass or two), or name another song first. `music_volume` and the music fades reach it like any song while it plays. `plays_once:` beside `loop_from:` is a friendly error, and a sound effect plays once whatever its Score says. It costs a game with such a song a load and a compare a frame, and a game with none nothing. The same part limits as a song block, and each mistake in a Score — a note before tick 0, a key that is not a note, a note naming an instrument on a square-wave part — is a friendly error naming the part and the note. Worked example: `test/all/vblank/audio/test_scores.rb`.
- `sfx = sound_effects :sfx, { hit: HIT, spark: SPARK }` then `sfx.play :hit` (or `sfx.play which`, a number the game works out) → a `SoundEffectList`: **Scores played once, over the song that is playing.** A sword hit, a door, a chest opening: each is a short run of notes the same shape as a tune, decoded from the same place, and each plays over the room's music without stopping it. It starts on the frame after it is asked for, the same step `play_song` takes, and it goes quiet at its end rather than coming round again. **`sfx.play :hit, this_frame: true` starts it where it is asked**, the way `play_song` takes the word: its first frame is played there, so every note is a frame sooner — and any effect a group is cutting off is cut off there too, so its voices are free for the new one. Nothing else sounding is moved on. That is for a game asking on another cartridge's own frames; see `play_song`. It costs a call where it is said and a short routine beside the player that walks the table once (measured at 180 bytes); a game that never says it emits neither. **Asked for again while it sounds, it starts again from its first note**; several different effects sound at once, each on its own. **Unless they share a group, and then they cut each other off** — `Score.new(..., group: :voice)`, a name, because a character has one voice: a hurt cry replaces a swing cry rather than playing over it. Asked for while another of its group is sounding or already asked for, an effect of at least that one's priority stops it (its voices go quiet on the next frame, a shaped recorded note falling away) and starts in its place; one of lower priority is not played at all, and the log does not say it started. A tie goes to the one asked for last, since that is the one the game wants now. One of a group asked for again while it sounds stops before it starts again, so a part whose first note comes later is quiet until then. Groups belong to the Scores, not the lists, so one list can hold every effect a game has and a number decoded from its data still picks one, whatever its group; an effect that names no group plays alongside any other. That is how a cartridge's own sound engine plays effects — each on one of a few players, one at a time — so a decoder hands over the player as the group. What a group costs is on the asking: the game holds the screen's interrupt off while it looks at each of the group, a load and a compare or three apiece, and asking for an effect from a list with no group in it is one store. A `songs` Score with a group is a friendly error. A number naming no effect plays nothing, and a written number or name the list does not have is a friendly error. **When an effect and the song, or two effects, want the same voice at once, the higher `priority:` sounds on it** — `Score.new(..., priority: 68)`, a whole number 0 to 255 on each Score, the song's too, because a cartridge's own sound engine carries one on every sequence and a decoder hands it straight over. The one that loses the voice carries on in time, silent there, and is heard again from its next note once the voice is free; the note it lost is not played late. A note at volume 0 lets the voice go, the same as a rest. On a tie an effect takes the voice from the song, so a game that says nothing about priority hears its effects, and between two effects the one declared first takes it. That tie is the framework's choice: a cartridge's own engine can settle a tie between its tune and an effect the other way, so music decoded from one with a tie in it wants the priorities made different. A song that stops or starts over leaves an effect's voice alone, and `music_volume` never reaches an effect, which keeps its own volume. **What an effect can play is every voice a song can: the two square voices, the wave voice, the noise voice and recordings** — a character's cry, a voice line, a hammer, a key appearing in a round tone. **A note on the wave voice sounds in its own waveform**, the effect's while the effect holds the voice and the song's again once the song's next note has it, whichever timbre or 32 steps each part names. The voice has room for one waveform, so a note puts its own in place first when another is there — on the frames the waveform changes and no others — and a tune that changes while an effect holds the voice waits for its first note to have the voice before its waveform goes in. On that voice a note too quiet for its five volumes lets the voice go, the same as a rest. A part that plays a recording (`Score::Part.new(plays: :grunt, notes: ...)`) sounds each note on a voice of the mixer at that note's pitch, the same as a song's recorded part, shaped the same way and its own volume whatever `music_volume` says. **When every mixer voice is busy, priority decides there too**: a note that finds no free voice and no note fading away takes the voice of the game's own sound playing longest (the game's sounds rank below every song and effect), and past that the voice of the lowest-priority note ranked below its own — so an effect over a song with a full mixer is heard, and cuts one of the song's notes short. With none of those the note is not played, and its part is heard again from its next note. Two parts of the same song or the same effect never take each other's voice, and a recorded note at volume 0 is a rest here too: it takes no voice. That is the rule a cartridge's own sound engine keeps, with two differences worth knowing when music comes from one: among notes fading away the quietest gives way first rather than the lowest priority, and the mixer here has 16 voices where a cartridge may set fewer, so a note that cartridge would drop can still play. `loop_from:` is a friendly error, since an effect plays once. An effect's Score takes `low_notes_intended: true` the same way a song's does (see `songs`). The interpreter's log says `[:sound_effect, name]` on the frame each one starts, so a game's own test can say which effect started when without the emulator, and `[:voice, channel, who, frequency, volume]` each time the song or an effect sets one of the console voices, which is who sounded on it; for the mixer, `sound_owners` says whose each sounding voice is — `:game`, `[:song, part]` or `[effect, part]` — and the console's `voices` say the same. **What it costs**: a game with no sound effects emits nothing new. One with them keeps a small table in the roomy memory, and the screen's interrupt walks it every frame, a load and a compare for an effect that is not sounding. An effect with something to do on a frame is handed to a routine of its own, kept in the cartridge: that routine is written out for every voice the effects use between them, so it does not take the console's quick memory from the game. An effect on a voice no other effect uses still costs the routine that answers the display a little when a song plays on that voice too, since the song then checks who holds it, and the first effect that plays a recording makes the mixer's voices rank against each other. A sounding effect costs a call there each frame, a check per voice it uses, and more on a frame one of its notes is due. A game whose effects play recordings adds a compare per busy voice when a note looks for one, and one whose effects play the wave voice a short call on each note there, to compare the waveform with the one in place, and the copy of a waveform on a frame it changes. `rom.profile` counts that time in the effects' own routine, apart from the routine that answers the display. Worked examples: `examples/jukebox.rb` (A and B play two effects on a piano over a tune whose big chord fills the mixer, one ranked above it and one below) and `test/all/vblank/dsl/test_sound_effects.rb`.
- `instrument :piano, from: "piano.wav", envelope: { release: 0.25 }, holds_from: 0.4` — **how a note on this recording starts and how it ENDS**, which is worth reading before you write any music at all, because the thing it fixes sounds like a bug in the recording. A note that simply stops leaves the speaker wherever the wave happened to be, and the jump from there to silence is a CLICK — louder than anything in the music, since a note cut at the top of its wave moves the speaker further in one step than the music ever does. And it comes and goes: a note that ends near the middle of its wave is silent about it. So it arrives as "sometimes there's a pop at the end of a lick", which is the worst kind of thing to be handed. Give the note a shape and it gets quieter over a few frames instead of stopping, and the click is gone. **Four words, all optional, and what you leave out stays as it was** — so `envelope: { release: 0.25 }` says the one thing most music needs: `attack` is how long it takes to reach full when the note starts, `decay` how long it then takes to fall back, `sustain` where it holds while the note lasts (a fraction of full, 0.0 to 1.0), and `release` how long it takes to fade away once the note ends. The three times are in seconds. A struck instrument — a piano, a bell — is `{ decay: 0.4, sustain: 0.3, release: 0.5 }`; a bowed or blown one climbs instead, `{ attack: 0.08, release: 0.15 }`. **Any release removes the click, however short** — the loudness slides across each frame's samples rather than stepping at the frame boundary, so even the fastest release on a retail cartridge (89, silent in six frames) ends a note without one. **A shaped note's loudness for a frame is a whole number of 256ths**, the loudness asked for times its level, the rest dropped (a loudness's odd 512th included) — which is how a cartridge's own sound engine works it out — so a quiet note falls silent while its level is still above nothing, on the frame the original game's does, and a quiet note whose shape holds it below full can be silent for the whole of its hold. A note held at full is exactly the loudness asked for. A note that comes before the last one has finished fading does not cut the fade off either (see the song line). **Or give the four numbers the console's own sound engine keeps**, 0 to 255 each, with `VBlank::Audio::Envelope.new(attack: 255, decay: 245, sustain: 180, release: 216)` — for music decoded out of another cartridge, whose instruments already carry them and need no converting (attack is ADDED to the level each frame; decay and release are what it is MULTIPLIED by, out of 256; sustain is where it holds). **What it costs** is one pass over the sounding voices once a frame, where the four numbers move the level, and one addition per sample per sounding voice in the mix, to slide the loudness there — and a game that shapes no note emits none of it.
- `instrument :piano, from: "piano.wav", holds_from: 0.4` — **where a held note reads back to**, so a note can OUTLAST its recording. A piano recorded for half a second and held for two seconds otherwise ends where the recording does, which is both too early and a click. Say where the body of the note begins — after the strike — and a long note reads round that point for as long as it lasts, so the attack is heard once and the note goes on. A time in seconds (a Float), or a sample number (a whole number), which is what a recording lifted out of another game arrives with. A point outside the recording is a friendly error naming the recording and its length. A recording that says nothing runs out as it always did. Both words work on `sample` too.
- `instrument :name, from: "piano.wav", note: :C4` (same `pcm:`/`from:`/`rate:`/`note:` as `sample`) → an `Instrument`: a recorded sample you play *notes* on, one recording covering the whole keyboard. `inst.play(:E4)` plays a note; `inst.play(:C4, :E4, :G4)` a chord (each note a voice, sounding together); `inst.play(:E4, volume: :half)` sets level; `inst.stop` silences it. Pure sugar over `sample.play(pitch:)` — each note is a pitched voice in the mixer. Trigger notes live from input (`pressed(:a).then { piano.play(:C4) }`), or let a song play it: `voice :melody, plays: :piano` (see `song`).
